Acute Care Physical Therapy
Clinical Discussion
Guideline
A framework for peer case discussion, centered on clinical reasoning and decision-making in acute care
Center the session on one or two clinical reasoning domains and explore them fully. Depth matters more than coverage.
Section 01Preparation
Case selection & preparation

Cases need not involve adverse events; complex and exemplary cases are often equally instructive.

Complex reasoning
Atypical presentations, diagnostic uncertainty, or competing comorbidities
Clinical decision points
Unexpected trajectories, escalation or de-escalation decisions, or major changes to the plan of care
Exemplary practice
Strong therapeutic alliance, creative problem-solving, or effective interprofessional collaboration
Safety events & near misses
Adverse events, near misses, or unexpected outcomes with system-level lessons
DataDe-identify all patient information before the session
RolesAssign a presenter and, if helpful, a content expert; circulate key literature in advance
PurposeDecide the session's purpose in advance: learning, quality improvement, or peer review
TimingA 30-minute session might allow about 3 minutes to open, 10 to present, 12 to discuss, and 5 to close
Giesbrecht & Au, Jt Comm J Qual Patient Saf 2016 · Brook et al., J Patient Saf 2024
Section 02Facilitation
Opening the discussion

The opening sets the tone. These three moves take a minute or two and make candid discussion possible.

Establish
Confidentiality & psychological safety
State it explicitly before the case begins. Clinicians are unlikely to share uncertainty, error, or disagreement unless it is clearly safe to do so. One sentence is enough, but it must be said.
Reframe toward Safety-II
Start with what went well
Safety-II examines how care usually succeeds, not only how it fails. Starting here shifts the group from defensive to curious and surfaces expertise that often goes unrecognized.
Name the purpose
Learning, quality improvement, or peer review
Naming the purpose tells participants how the discussion will be used, which shapes how candid they can be.
Edmondson, Adm Sci Q 1999 · Hollnagel, Wears & Braithwaite 2015 · Brook et al., J Patient Saf 2024

Section 03 — continuedPresentation
ICF examination snapshot

Consider findings across all ICF components; each informs the others.

Body functions & structures
Strength, ROM, cardiopulmonary status, tone, cognition, pain, relevant labs and vitals
Activities
Bed mobility, transfers, gait, stairs; validated measures for the population and context
Participation
Life roles and community participation the patient aims to resume
Contextual factors
Environmental (home setup, caregiver capacity, discharge options) and personal (values, priorities, coping)
WHO ICF 2001 · Atkinson & Nixon-Cave, Phys Ther 2011 · Davenport, Physiother Res Int 2015

Section 05Complexity
Navigating complexity & uncertainty

Acute care practice involves ill-structured problems, incomplete information, and evolving clinical pictures.

AmbiguityWhen was the clinical picture most ambiguous, and how did you manage the uncertainty?
Competing prioritiesHow did you weigh competing priorities, such as medical stability versus early mobilization, or patient preference versus clinical risk?
New informationHow did new information (labs, imaging, surgical updates) change your reasoning?
Pattern recognitionWhere did pattern recognition help, and where might it have led to bias or premature closure?
ConstraintsWhich constraints (staffing, time, equipment, discharge options) shaped your decisions, and how did you work within them?
Thinking modeWhen did you rely on fast, intuitive judgment versus slow, deliberate analysis, and was each appropriate?
Smith, Higgs & Ellis, Physiother Res Int 2008 · Croskerry, Acad Med 2009 · Huhn et al., Phys Ther 2019
Section 06Treatment
Plan of care

Prompts follow the episode of care; discharge planning runs throughout.

Evaluation
Which examination findings informed your prognosis and plan of care, and which carried the most weight?
How were problems prioritized, and which were deferred to the next level of care?
Early sessions
What interventions were selected, and what evidence supported them?
How were dose, intensity, and progression set?
Ongoing
Where did the plan change in real time, and what prompted it?
How was patient and caregiver education built in, and how was learning confirmed?
Throughout
When did discharge planning begin, and what shaped the recommended disposition?

Section 09Discussion
Moderator prompts

A designated moderator is optional. If the group chooses one, ideally it is a content expert in the case's clinical area, who can ask informed follow-up questions. The prompts below work with or without one.

Clinical reasoning
  • “Walk us through your thinking at the point you were most uncertain.”
  • “What would have changed your approach, and why?”
  • “Where did your reasoning shift, and what prompted it?”
Complexity & systems
  • “Is this a recurring pattern in this patient population?”
  • “What does this case reveal about how we communicate as a team?”
  • “What would need to change in practice, workflow, or knowledge to handle a similar case better?”
  • “What would you tell a newer colleague about this type of case?”
Giesbrecht & Au, Jt Comm J Qual Patient Saf 2016 · Brook et al., J Patient Saf 2024 · Lai et al., Fam Pract Manag 2023
Section 10Close
Closing & action items

A good close is brief and concrete: two named lessons and one assigned action is a strong outcome for a single session.

Synthesize learning
Name one reasoning lesson and one systems lesson before closing
Assign actions
An owner, a specific task, and a deadline
Document & track
Record outcomes, review progress at the next session, and link to department quality metrics where relevant
Brook et al., J Patient Saf 2024 · Lai et al., Fam Pract Manag 2023
Section 10 — continuedClose
Weaker and stronger closes

Illustrated with a missed-opportunity version of the predictive vignette, in which the recommendation stays home until the score reaches 13.

Weaker
Stronger
Reasoning lesson
“We should reassess discharge plans more often.”
“When the AM‑PAC fell from 21 to 16, that was the point to revise the discharge estimate and tell case management, not after a third score confirmed it.”
Systems lesson
“Discharge planning started too late.”
“Nothing in our workflow links a declining 6‑Clicks score to a disposition review, so the update depended on individual judgment.”
Action
“We should communicate better with case management.”
“The presenting therapist and unit lead will propose a score-change threshold that triggers an updated recommendation to case management, and bring it to the next session.”
